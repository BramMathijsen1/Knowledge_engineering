Method

We followed eXtreme Design: start from stories, turn them into competency questions, match the questions to ontology patterns, and answer each question with a SPARQL query.

Stories

  • A music historian wants to know if the documented roots of punk match what punk bands say influenced them.
  • A student wants to follow the path from an old blues singer to a band of the 1990s.
  • A journalist reads that an artist was "influenced by" another one and wants to know who said so.
  • A musician wants to know if the synthesizer came into hip hop through its parent genres or through the artists hip hop musicians listened to.

Competency questions

  1. What are the roots of a genre?
  2. Which genres have the most descendants?
  3. Which fusion genres join two genres that had no link before?
  4. Which form does a genre start in, and how does it change?
  5. Which artists influenced the most other artists, and across how many genres?
  6. Is there a chain of influence between two artists?
  7. Whose influence is declared by artists themselves, and whose is only stated by others?
  8. Which genres do sampled and covered recordings come from and go to?
  9. If we add up artist influence by genre, which routes do we get?
  10. Do those routes follow the family tree of genres?
  11. Which links in the family tree have no artists behind them?
  12. What does the graph say about a genre?
  13. What instruments do the artists of each genre play?
  14. Which instruments does a genre use that none of the genres it comes from use?
  15. Does an artist play the instruments of their influences, of their genre's roots, both, or neither?

Questions the graph can't answer: what an influence sounds like (we have no audio features), and when an influence happened (claims have no dates).

Why the data needs curating

Raw linked data can't be used as it is. These problems shaped the project:

  • DBpedia has genre links but almost no influence links. So genres come from DBpedia and influence from other sources.
  • The raw genre links form loops. In all of DBpedia, 332 genres are in one big loop, so "ancestor of" means almost nothing. On our chosen genres there are no loops left.
  • Some links are simply wrong. For example, DBpedia connects trap to soul, but trap is decades younger. Dropped links stay in lineage.csv with the reason.
  • Wikidata has gaps and mistakes. Among our artists it has no influences at all for Kendrick Lamar or Miles Davis, and only one each for Beyoncé and Radiohead. So text extraction is necessary. It also says Sister Rosetta Tharpe was influenced by Little Richard, which is backwards: she started about 30 years before him. Comparing the years when the artists started finds errors like this.
  • Artists have many genres. If we use them all, nearly everything looks connected. So each artist has one primary genre, listed in artists.csv.

How to read the route labels

Reversed doesn't mean wrong. The family tree shows where a genre came from, but older genres don't stop when a newer one appears. Kendrick Lamar is a hip hop artist influenced by Tupac Shakur, a gangsta rap artist. Gangsta rap comes from hip hop, so this route counts as reversed, and it is still true.

Hidden doesn't mean secret. It only means our family tree has no link there. Hard rock to heavy metal comes out as hidden because DBpedia lists no link between them, even though the two are clearly close.

Evidence kinds

Each influence statement says how we know about it. Declared: the influenced artist says it. Attributed: someone else says it. Unsourced: it comes from Wikidata and we did not trace who claimed it. Sampling and Cover version: the influence can be heard in a recording.

Instruments

Each artist plays one or more instruments. For solo artists the data comes from Wikidata. For bands it comes from MusicBrainz, which says which instruments each member played in that band. When MusicBrainz has no instruments for a band, we use the instruments of its members from Wikidata, which covers their whole career. The source column in artist_instruments.csv says which one was used.

The many instrument names in the sources (for example "Gibson Les Paul" or "drums (drum set)") are mapped to a short list in instruments.csv. An instrument stays in the list only if at least three of our artists play it.

The graph does not store which instruments a genre uses. The queries work it out from the artists. In queries 15 and 16 a genre uses an instrument when at least two of its artists play it, so one unusual artist does not decide it. Query 16 then lists the instruments that no artist of the parent genres plays. Query 17 asks, for each instrument an artist plays, whether an artist who influenced them plays it too, and whether an artist of a parent genre plays it. Both checks look for at least one artist, so both sides are treated the same way. Query 17 leaves out the voice, because almost everyone sings.

Ontology patterns

PatternHow we use it
Situation (n-ary relation)riff:Influence holds the influencer, the influenced artist, the evidence and the source. A plain link can't hold all of this.
Identification keyowl:hasKey on riff:Influence: same artists, same evidence kind and same source mean the same statement.
Property chainmm:isInfluencedBy is defined from riff:Influence with a property chain. We also add it with a CONSTRUCT query, so queries work without a reasoner.
Transitionriff:GenrePhase gives the form of a genre at each step of its history.
Constructive queryQuery 9 builds riff:GenreRoute from the graph.
Avoiding an anti-patternSubgenres use skos:broader, not rdfs:subClassOf. Genres are individuals, because hard rock is not a kind of rock in the logical sense.

Influence is not transitive. If A influenced B and B influenced C, A did not have to influence C. So we don't declare the property transitive. We ask for chains with + in SPARQL.

Limits

  • We chose the genres and artists ourselves, about 3 to 5 artists per genre. Other choices would give other numbers.
  • Artists with no influence link in our sources were left out, including Charlie Parker and Joan Baez. That shows a gap in the sources, not that they had no influence. The jungle genre has no artists left for the same reason.
  • Instrument data is uneven. Some artists have no instruments in our sources, such as Beyoncé, the Sex Pistols and most of the techno artists. Sources also rarely say whether a guitar is electric or acoustic, so we only have "guitar".
  • For a solo artist we only know their own instruments, not those of their backing band. The older genres in our list are mostly solo singers, so query 16 can report drums or bass as new in rock or funk when they are not.
  • Query 17 shows that an artist plays the same instrument as their influences, not that they took it from them. Guitar, bass and drums are shared by almost everyone, so the interesting rows are the rarer instruments.
  • Wikidata and Wikipedia say much more about rock than about older music or about hip hop. A link with no artists behind it may only mean our sources are thin there.
  • Many routes with no link are between close genres, so they show gaps in the tree more than discoveries.
  • The years for genres are rough estimates from periods like "late 1960s".
  • Genre forms are only filled in for genres named in Regev's review of Lena's book.
  • We chose the sampled recordings, so query 8 describes our selection. It can't test what Bryan and Wang found.

Run it yourself

pip install -r requirements.txt
python scripts/build_graph.py
python scripts/run_queries.py
python scripts/build_site.py

To use a SPARQL endpoint, load graph/graph.ttl and ontology/riff.ttl into Apache Jena Fuseki on your computer.
